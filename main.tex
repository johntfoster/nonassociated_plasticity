\title{Apparent non-associative plasticity from true deformation and distension}
\author{John T. Foster}
\date{August 18, 2026}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Pressure-dependent solids commonly use a yield function and a distinct plastic
potential to represent friction and dilatancy.  We develop an alternative
description in which isochoric true-plastic deformation and plastic distension
are separate internal mechanisms of a single solid phase.  The finite-deformation
kinematics are defined by \(\mbf F=\mbf A\bar{\mbf F}\), with multiplicative
elastic--plastic splits of both factors.  Exploitation of the isothermal free-energy
inequality identifies a deviatoric Mandel-type stress conjugate to true-plastic
flow and a mean stress conjugate to logarithmic plastic distension.  Independent
normality laws on a product elastic domain are associated in this generalized
force space, although their combined observable strain rate need not be normal
to either projected surface.  We prove that this observation alone does not
establish equivalence to a classical one-surface non-associated model.

An exact pressure-dependent specialization is then constructed from a
stress-dependent dissipation potential and a dilatancy constraint.  In the
small-strain limit it gives the Drucker--Prager yield condition
\(q=Mp\), the flow direction generated by \(g=q-\beta p\), and the identity
\(M=\mu+\beta\), where \(\mu p\dot\gamma\) is the dissipated power.  The same
construction lifts directly to the finite-deformation true-plastic and
distension rates.  The result resolves the apparent non-associativity by
restoring normality in the enlarged generalized-force space while retaining a
non-normal reduced description in true-stress space.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Plasticity models for frictional materials frequently distinguish the surface
that activates plastic loading from the potential that determines the plastic
flow direction.  The difference permits the friction angle and dilation angle
to be calibrated independently.  It also places the flow rule outside the
classical normality structure of a single yield surface in true-stress space.

Thermodynamic formulations provide a larger set of work-conjugate variables.
Collins and Houlsby showed that normality in generalized-stress space can
produce non-normal irreversible rates after the constitutive equations are
expressed in true-stress space, and they gave a stress-dependent dissipation
construction for Drucker--Prager plasticity
\cite[Secs.~4--5]{collins1997}.  Independently, Drumheller's mixture kinematics
split deformation into true deformation and distension, with elastic--plastic
factors and distinct plastic powers for the two mechanisms
\cite[Secs.~3 and 8]{drumheller2000}.  These results suggest a direct physical
interpretation of pressure-dependent plastic flow: shear rearrangement changes
the true deformation, while compaction or dilation changes distension.

We develop that interpretation for one solid phase.  The central result has two
parts.  First, separate true-plastic and distension yield mechanisms define an
associated multi-mechanism theory in their complete generalized-force space.
Projection onto one observed yield surface can make the combined rate appear
non-normal, but a generic pair of independent surfaces is not globally
equivalent to a prescribed one-surface non-associated law.  Second, an exact
Drucker--Prager reduction follows when the two kinematic mechanisms share an
activation rate through a dilatancy constraint and a stress-dependent
dissipation potential.  This coupling fixes the multiplier ratio and produces
the classical yield slope and plastic-potential slope as distinct quantities.

Section~\ref{sec:kinematics} defines the finite-deformation split.
Section~\ref{sec:thermodynamics} derives the conjugate forces and plastic
dissipation.  Section~\ref{sec:two_mechanisms} states the associated
two-mechanism theory and its projection.  Section~\ref{sec:drucker_prager}
gives the exact small-strain pressure-dependent example, and
Section~\ref{sec:finite_lift} carries it back to finite deformation.

\section{Single-phase kinematics}
\label{sec:kinematics}

Let \(\bs\chi(\mbf X,t)\) be the motion of a solid body from a reference point
\(\mbf X\) to \(\mbf x\).  Its deformation gradient and Jacobian are
\begin{equation}
 \mbf F=\frac{\partial\bs\chi}{\partial\mbf X},
 \qquad
 J=\det\mbf F>0.
 \label{eq:motion}
\end{equation}
The deformation is decomposed into distension and true deformation,
\begin{equation}
 \mbf F=\mbf A\bar{\mbf F},
 \qquad
 J=a\bar J,
 \qquad
 a=\det\mbf A,
 \qquad
 \bar J=\det\bar{\mbf F}.
 \label{eq:distension_split}
\end{equation}
The tensor \(\bar{\mbf F}\) traces deformation of the solid material, while
\(\mbf A\) accommodates the surrounding volume assigned to that material.
This split is the single-phase specialization of the true-deformation and
distension kinematics introduced for immiscible mixtures by Drumheller
\cite[Secs.~3 and 8.2]{drumheller2000}.

Each factor has an elastic--plastic split,
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:plastic_splits}
\begin{align}
 \mbf A&=\mbf A^e\mbf A^p,
 \label{eq:distension_plastic_split}\\
 \bar{\mbf F}&=\bar{\mbf F}^{e}\bar{\mbf F}^{p},
 \qquad
 \det\bar{\mbf F}^{p}=1.
 \label{eq:true_plastic_split}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
The last condition assigns plastic volume change to \(\mbf A^p\) and makes
true-plastic flow isochoric.  Drumheller's finite-deformation construction uses
the same distinction: the true-plastic factor is incompressible, whereas the
plastic-distension factor may change volume
\cite[eqs.~(71)--(74)]{drumheller2000}.

The pressure-dependent specialization uses isotropic distension,
\begin{equation}
 \mbf A=a^{1/3}\mbf I,
 \qquad
 a=a^e a^p,
 \qquad
 \zeta=\ln a,
 \qquad
 \zeta=\zeta^e+\zeta^p.
 \label{eq:isotropic_distension}
\end{equation}
The velocity gradient then separates into a spherical distension rate and the
true-deformation rate,
\begin{equation}
 \mbf L=\dot{\mbf F}\mbf F^{-1}
 =\frac{\dot\zeta}{3}\mbf I
 +\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}\bar{\mbf F}^{-1}.
 \label{eq:velocity_gradient}
\end{equation}
Define the true-plastic velocity gradient by
\begin{equation}
 \bar{\mbf L}^{p}
 =\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}^{p}(\bar{\mbf F}^{p})^{-1},
 \qquad
 \tr\bar{\mbf L}^{p}=0.
 \label{eq:true_plastic_rate}
\end{equation}
Differentiation of \eqref{eq:true_plastic_split} gives
\begin{equation}
 \dot{\bar{\mbf F}}\bar{\mbf F}^{-1}
 =\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}^{e}(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^{-1}
 +\bar{\mbf F}^{e}\bar{\mbf L}^{p}
 (\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^{-1}.
 \label{eq:true_rate_split}
\end{equation}

\section{Free-energy restriction and plastic power}
\label{sec:thermodynamics}

Let \(\Psi\) be the isothermal Helmholtz free energy per unit reference volume.
For the kinematic variables above and hardening variables
\(\kappa_d,\kappa_v\), take
\begin{equation}
 \Psi=\widehat\Psi
 \left(
 \bar{\mbf F}^{e},\zeta^e,\kappa_d,\kappa_v
 \right).
 \label{eq:free_energy}
\end{equation}
We assume material frame indifference, so superposed rotations of
\(\bar{\mbf F}^{e}\) leave \(\widehat\Psi\) unchanged, and use balance of
angular momentum to take \(\bs\tau\) symmetric.  The pressure-dependent
specialization below also assumes an isotropic elastic response; its
Mandel-type stress is then symmetric.
The local free-energy inequality is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal D
 =\mbf P:\dot{\mbf F}-\dot\Psi
 \ge 0,
 \label{eq:free_energy_inequality}
\end{equation}
where \(\mbf P\) is the first Piola stress.  Introduce the Kirchhoff stress
\(\bs\tau=\mbf P\mbf F^T\).  Equations
\eqref{eq:velocity_gradient} and \eqref{eq:true_rate_split} give the mechanical
power
\begin{align}
 \mbf P:\dot{\mbf F}
 ={}&\frac{1}{3}\tr(\bs\tau)\dot\zeta
 +\left[\bs\tau(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^{-T}\right]
 :\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}^{e}
 \notag\\
 &+\left[(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^T\bs\tau
 (\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^{-T}\right]:\bar{\mbf L}^{p}.
 \label{eq:mechanical_power_split}
\end{align}
The free-energy rate is
\begin{align}
 \dot\Psi={}&
 \frac{\partial\widehat\Psi}{\partial\bar{\mbf F}^{e}}
 :\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}^{e}
 +\frac{\partial\widehat\Psi}{\partial\zeta^e}
 \left(\dot\zeta-\dot\zeta^p\right)
 \notag\\
 &+\frac{\partial\widehat\Psi}{\partial\kappa_d}\dot\kappa_d
 +\frac{\partial\widehat\Psi}{\partial\kappa_v}\dot\kappa_v.
 \label{eq:free_energy_rate}
\end{align}
Independent elastic rates in \eqref{eq:free_energy_inequality} produce the
reversible restrictions
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:reversible_restrictions}
\begin{align}
 \frac{\partial\widehat\Psi}{\partial\bar{\mbf F}^{e}}
 &=\bs\tau(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^{-T},
 \label{eq:true_stress_restriction}\\
 \frac{\partial\widehat\Psi}{\partial\zeta^e}
 &=\frac{1}{3}\tr\bs\tau.
 \label{eq:distension_stress_restriction}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

Define the compression-positive mean Kirchhoff stress, the deviatoric
true-plastic driving stress, and the hardening forces by
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:conjugate_forces}
\begin{align}
 p&=-\frac{1}{3}\tr\bs\tau,
 \label{eq:mean_stress}\\
 \mbf M&=\dev\left[
 (\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^T\bs\tau
 (\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^{-T}
 \right],
 \label{eq:mandel_stress}\\
 Q_d&=-\frac{\partial\widehat\Psi}{\partial\kappa_d},
 \qquad
 Q_v=-\frac{\partial\widehat\Psi}{\partial\kappa_v}.
 \label{eq:hardening_forces}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Substitution of \eqref{eq:reversible_restrictions} into
\eqref{eq:free_energy_inequality} leaves
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal D^p
 =\mbf M:\bar{\mbf L}^{p}
 -p\dot\zeta^p
 +Q_d\dot\kappa_d+Q_v\dot\kappa_v
 \ge0.
 \label{eq:plastic_dissipation}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:plastic_dissipation} is the controlling result.  The
isochoric rate \(\bar{\mbf L}^{p}\) is conjugate to \(\mbf M\), while
logarithmic plastic distension \(\dot\zeta^p=\dot a^p/a^p\) is conjugate to
\(-p\).  The two powers are the single-phase counterparts of the true-plastic
and plastic-distension powers obtained in Drumheller's constitutive theory
\cite[eqs.~(108)--(112)]{drumheller2000}.

\section{Associated mechanisms and projection}
\label{sec:two_mechanisms}

This section first considers independent rate-independent mechanisms.  Define
the equivalent true-plastic stress and rate by
\begin{equation}
 q=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\lVert\mbf M\rVert,
 \qquad
 \dot\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}
 \lVert\bar{\mbf L}^{p}\rVert.
 \label{eq:equivalent_quantities}
\end{equation}
For coaxial loading with \(q>0\), the normal direction is
\begin{equation}
 \mbf N=\frac{3}{2}\frac{\mbf M}{q},
 \qquad
 \bar{\mbf L}^{p}=\dot\gamma\mbf N,
 \qquad
 \mbf M:\bar{\mbf L}^{p}=q\dot\gamma.
 \label{eq:deviatoric_normal}
\end{equation}

A simple product elastic domain is defined by
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:product_yield_conditions}
\begin{align}
 f_d(q,\kappa_d)&=q-Y(\kappa_d)\le0,
 \label{eq:true_yield}\\
 f_v(-p,\kappa_v)&=|p|-K(\kappa_v)\le0,
 \label{eq:distension_yield}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where \(Y\ge0\) and \(K\ge0\).  Associated flow and hardening are
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:product_flow_rules}
\begin{align}
 \bar{\mbf L}^{p}
 &=\dot\lambda_d\frac{\partial f_d}{\partial\mbf M},
 \qquad
 \dot\kappa_d=\dot\lambda_d h_d,
 \label{eq:true_associated_flow}\\
 \dot\zeta^p
 &=\dot\lambda_v\frac{\partial f_v}{\partial(-p)},
 \qquad
 \dot\kappa_v=\dot\lambda_v h_v,
 \label{eq:distension_associated_flow}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
with \(h_d,h_v\ge0\).  Each multiplier satisfies the Kuhn--Tucker conditions
\begin{equation}
 \dot\lambda_i\ge0,
 \qquad
 f_i\le0,
 \qquad
 \dot\lambda_i f_i=0,
 \qquad
 i\in\{d,v\}.
 \label{eq:kuhn_tucker}
\end{equation}
During active loading the consistency equations are
\begin{equation}
 \dot f_i=0
 \quad\text{when}\quad
 \dot\lambda_i>0,
 \qquad
 i\in\{d,v\}.
 \label{eq:consistency}
\end{equation}
They determine the active multipliers together with the stress update and the
hardening laws in \eqref{eq:product_flow_rules}.

For energetic hardening, admissibility requires the active hardening powers to
be included.  A transparent sufficient specialization sets
\begin{equation}
 Q_d h_d=0,
 \qquad
 Q_v h_v=0,
 \label{eq:dissipative_hardening_specialization}
\end{equation}
so that the hardening variables change the yield strengths without storing
energy.  The active yield conditions and \eqref{eq:product_flow_rules} then
give
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal D^p
 =Y\dot\lambda_d+K\dot\lambda_v
 \ge0.
 \label{eq:product_dissipation_proof}
\end{equation}
More general energetic hardening is admissible when the last two terms in
\eqref{eq:plastic_dissipation} preserve this inequality.

The product-domain theory has a precise projection property.  In the
small-strain limit, let \(\dot{\bs\varepsilon}^{p}_{d}\) denote the traceless
true-plastic strain rate.  The observable plastic strain rate is
\begin{equation}
 \dot{\bs\varepsilon}^{p}
 =\dot{\bs\varepsilon}^{p}_{d}
 +\frac{1}{3}\dot\zeta^p\mbf I.
 \label{eq:observable_plastic_rate}
\end{equation}
If both mechanisms are active, substitution of
\eqref{eq:product_flow_rules} gives
\begin{equation}
 \dot{\bs\varepsilon}^{p}
 =\dot\lambda_d
 \frac{\partial f_d}{\partial\mbf M}
 +\frac{\dot\lambda_v}{3}
 \frac{\partial f_v}{\partial(-p)}\mbf I.
 \label{eq:projected_product_flow}
\end{equation}
The second term is not normal to the projected deviatoric surface
\(f_d=0\).  A reduced observer who retains that surface but measures the total
rate \eqref{eq:observable_plastic_rate} therefore sees an additional
volumetric contribution.

\begin{proposition}[Projection limitation]
\label{prop:projection_limitation}
A regular product domain with two independent active surfaces is not locally
equivalent to a regular one-surface non-associated model unless an additional
constitutive relation reduces the active conditions and fixes the ratio of the
two plastic multipliers.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
At a point where both mechanisms are active, \(f_d=0\) and \(f_v=0\) impose two
independent conditions.  Their intersection has codimension two when their
gradients are independent, whereas a regular one-surface yield locus has
codimension one.  Moreover, \eqref{eq:consistency} determines
\(\dot\lambda_d\) and \(\dot\lambda_v\) through two consistency equations; it
does not generally produce the multiplier ratio required by a chosen plastic
potential.  A shared activation condition or an explicit constitutive coupling
is therefore necessary for exact equivalence.
\end{proof}

There is also a power restriction.  Under compression \(p>0\), dilation has
\(\dot\zeta^p>0\), so its contribution \(-p\dot\zeta^p\) in
\eqref{eq:plastic_dissipation} is negative.  A model that requires the
distension power to be nonnegative separately admits compaction under
compression but excludes dilatancy.  Frictional dilation must instead be
supported by the positive true-plastic power in the combined inequality.

\section{Exact pressure-dependent specialization}
\label{sec:drucker_prager}

The exact small-strain construction follows from the combined plastic power
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal D^p=q\dot\gamma-p\dot\zeta^p.
 \label{eq:small_strain_power}
\end{equation}
Take compression as positive, restrict the example to \(p\ge0\), and let
\(\dot\gamma\ge0\).  The dilatancy relation is the kinematic constraint
\begin{equation}
 \dot\zeta^p=\beta\dot\gamma,
 \qquad
 \beta\ge0.
 \label{eq:dilatancy_constraint}
\end{equation}
The coefficient \(\beta\) is the dilation slope.  Choose the
stress-dependent, positively homogeneous dissipation potential
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal R(p;\dot\gamma,\dot\zeta^p)
 =\mu p\dot\gamma
 +I_{\mathcal C}(\dot\gamma,\dot\zeta^p),
 \qquad
 \mu\ge0,
 \label{eq:dissipation_potential}
\end{equation}
where the indicator is zero on
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal C
 =\left\{
 (\dot\gamma,\dot\zeta^p):
 \dot\gamma\ge0,
 \ \dot\zeta^p=\beta\dot\gamma
 \right\}
 \label{eq:admissible_rate_cone}
\end{equation}
and is \(+\infty\) elsewhere.  This construction adapts the
stress-dependent dissipation and dilation constraint used by Collins and
Houlsby for their Drucker--Prager example
\cite[eqs.~(5.8)--(5.14)]{collins1997}.
The stress \(p\) is held fixed as a passive parameter when the potential is
differentiated with respect to the rates.

Introduce a multiplier \(\Lambda\) for the equality in
\eqref{eq:dilatancy_constraint}.  On an active ray with
\(\dot\gamma>0\), the augmented potential is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal R^*
 =\mu p\dot\gamma
 +\Lambda\left(\dot\zeta^p-\beta\dot\gamma\right).
 \label{eq:augmented_potential}
\end{equation}
Let \(\chi_q\) and \(\chi_v\) be generalized forces conjugate to
\(\dot\gamma\) and \(\dot\zeta^p\), respectively.  Normality in the generalized
space gives
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:generalized_forces}
\begin{align}
 \chi_q&=\frac{\partial\mathcal R^*}{\partial\dot\gamma}
 =\mu p-\beta\Lambda,
 \label{eq:generalized_shear_force}\\
 \chi_v&=\frac{\partial\mathcal R^*}{\partial\dot\zeta^p}
 =\Lambda.
 \label{eq:generalized_volume_force}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Elimination of \(\Lambda\) gives the generalized yield condition
\begin{equation}
 f_g(\chi_q,\chi_v;p)
 =\chi_q+\beta\chi_v-\mu p
 \le0.
 \label{eq:generalized_yield}
\end{equation}
Its outward normal is \((1,\beta)\), exactly the rate direction in
\eqref{eq:dilatancy_constraint}.  The Kuhn--Tucker and consistency conditions
are
\begin{equation}
 \dot\lambda\ge0,
 \qquad
 f_g\le0,
 \qquad
 \dot\lambda f_g=0,
 \qquad
 \dot f_g=0\ \text{when}\ \dot\lambda>0,
 \label{eq:generalized_loading_conditions}
\end{equation}
with \(\dot\gamma=\dot\lambda\) and
\(\dot\zeta^p=\beta\dot\lambda\).  The theory is therefore associated in the
generalized-force space.

For zero back stress, the forces entering the physical power
\eqref{eq:small_strain_power} are
\begin{equation}
 \chi_q=q,
 \qquad
 \chi_v=-p.
 \label{eq:true_force_identification}
\end{equation}
Substitution into \eqref{eq:generalized_yield} produces the true-stress yield
condition
\begin{equation}
 f(p,q)=q-Mp\le0,
 \qquad
 M=\mu+\beta.
 \label{eq:drucker_prager_yield}
\end{equation}
The observable plastic rate is generated in true-stress space by
\begin{equation}
 g(p,q)=q-\beta p,
 \qquad
 \dot{\bs\varepsilon}^{p}
 =\dot\lambda\frac{\partial g}{\partial\bs\sigma}.
 \label{eq:plastic_potential}
\end{equation}
To verify the invariant normalization, define
\begin{equation}
 p=-\frac{1}{3}\tr\bs\sigma,
 \qquad
 \mbf s=\dev\bs\sigma,
 \qquad
 q=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\lVert\mbf s\rVert.
 \label{eq:small_strain_invariants}
\end{equation}
Then \eqref{eq:plastic_potential} gives
\begin{equation}
 \dot{\bs\varepsilon}^{p}
 =\dot\lambda\left(
 \frac{3}{2}\frac{\mbf s}{q}
 +\frac{\beta}{3}\mbf I
 \right),
 \qquad
 \dot\gamma=\dot\lambda,
 \qquad
 \tr\dot{\bs\varepsilon}^{p}=\beta\dot\lambda.
 \label{eq:drucker_prager_flow}
\end{equation}
The normal to \(f=0\) would contain \(M\mbf I/3\).  The actual volumetric
coefficient is \(\beta\), so the reduced true-stress description is
non-associated whenever \(\mu>0\).

The dissipation follows by direct substitution, not by an independent
postulate.  On the active surface,
\begin{align}
 \mathcal D^p
 &=q\dot\gamma-p\dot\zeta^p
 \notag\\
 &=\left(M-\beta\right)p\dot\gamma
 \notag\\
 &=\mu p\dot\gamma
 \ge0.
 \label{eq:drucker_prager_dissipation}
\end{align}
Thus frictional strength decomposes into the dilation slope \(\beta\) and the
dissipative slope \(\mu\).  Associated flow in true-stress space occurs only
for \(\mu=0\), for which this idealized rate-independent mechanism dissipates
no power.  Collins and Houlsby obtain the same separation in their generalized
stress construction \cite[eq.~(5.14)]{collins1997}.

\begin{proposition}[Exact Drucker--Prager reduction]
\label{prop:exact_dp_reduction}
For \(p\ge0\), \(\dot\gamma\ge0\), and constants \(\mu,\beta\ge0\), the
generalized potential \eqref{eq:dissipation_potential} produces the
Drucker--Prager surface \eqref{eq:drucker_prager_yield}, the flow rule
\eqref{eq:drucker_prager_flow}, and the nonnegative dissipation
\eqref{eq:drucker_prager_dissipation}.  The reduced true-stress flow is
non-associated precisely when \(\mu>0\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Equations \eqref{eq:generalized_forces} eliminate \(\Lambda\) to give
\eqref{eq:generalized_yield}.  The physical-force identification
\eqref{eq:true_force_identification} gives
\(M=\mu+\beta\) in \eqref{eq:drucker_prager_yield}.  Normality to the
generalized surface gives the rate ratio in \eqref{eq:dilatancy_constraint},
which is the true-stress flow \eqref{eq:drucker_prager_flow}.  Direct
substitution gives \eqref{eq:drucker_prager_dissipation}.  The normal to the
true-stress yield surface has volumetric slope \(M\), while the flow has slope
\(\beta\); these slopes differ exactly when \(\mu=M-\beta>0\).
\end{proof}

The equivalence is exact under the assumptions used above: coaxial isotropic
response, monotone loading with \(p\ge0\) and \(\dot\gamma\ge0\), constant
\(\mu\) and \(\beta\), zero cohesion, and no back stress.  Cohesion and
hardening may be added by replacing \(\mu p\) with a nonnegative
state-dependent strength and supplying its evolution.  Changes of loading
direction, corners, cyclic reversal, and anisotropy require the corresponding
tensor-valued rate potential and active-set rules.  The product-domain model of
Section~\ref{sec:two_mechanisms} and the coupled model in this section are
therefore distinct constitutive choices.

\section{Finite-deformation lift}
\label{sec:finite_lift}

The pressure-dependent construction lifts to finite deformation through the
conjugate pairs already derived in \eqref{eq:plastic_dissipation}.  Neglect
energetic hardening for the present specialization and define
\begin{equation}
 q=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\lVert\mbf M\rVert,
 \qquad
 \mbf N=\frac{3}{2}\frac{\mbf M}{q}.
 \label{eq:finite_equivalent_stress}
\end{equation}
The finite-deformation evolution equations are
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:finite_flow_rules}
\begin{align}
 \dot{\bar{\mbf F}}^{p}(\bar{\mbf F}^{p})^{-1}
 &=\dot\lambda\mbf N,
 \label{eq:finite_true_flow}\\
 \frac{\dot a^p}{a^p}
 &=\dot\zeta^p=\beta\dot\lambda.
 \label{eq:finite_distension_flow}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Because \(\tr\mbf N=0\), the first equation preserves
\(\det\bar{\mbf F}^{p}=1\).  The second evolves plastic distension.  Together
they update the factors in
\begin{equation}
 \mbf F
 =\left(a^e a^p\right)^{1/3}\mbf I
 \bar{\mbf F}^{e}\bar{\mbf F}^{p}.
 \label{eq:finite_total_split}
\end{equation}

Use the yield condition and loading rules
\begin{equation}
 f(\mbf M,p)=q-(\mu+\beta)p\le0,
 \qquad
 \dot\lambda\ge0,
 \qquad
 \dot\lambda f=0,
 \qquad
 \dot f=0\ \text{when}\ \dot\lambda>0.
 \label{eq:finite_loading_conditions}
\end{equation}
Substitution of \eqref{eq:finite_flow_rules} and
\eqref{eq:finite_loading_conditions} into the finite-deformation plastic power
gives
\begin{align}
 \mathcal D^p
 &=\mbf M:
 \left[
 \dot{\bar{\mbf F}}^{p}(\bar{\mbf F}^{p})^{-1}
 \right]
 -p\frac{\dot a^p}{a^p}
 \notag\\
 &=q\dot\lambda-p\beta\dot\lambda
 \notag\\
 &=\mu p\dot\lambda
 \ge0.
 \label{eq:finite_dissipation_proof}
\end{align}
The proof uses the stresses conjugate to the actual internal rates.  The
finite-deformation surface is therefore a Drucker--Prager-type condition in
\((p,q)\) formed from the Kirchhoff mean stress and the Mandel-type
true-plastic stress.  It coincides with the conventional Cauchy-stress form in
the small-strain limit.  Away from that limit, an exact identification with a
particular spatial Drucker--Prager implementation requires the chosen stress
measure and objective update to be mapped into \(\mbf M\) and \(p\).

The generalized associated structure is retained at finite deformation.  Let
\(\chi_q\) and \(\chi_v\) remain conjugate to \(\dot\lambda\) and
\(\dot\zeta^p\).  The generalized surface, physical-force identification, and
normal direction are
\begin{equation}
 \chi_q+\beta\chi_v-\mu p=0,
 \qquad
 \chi_q=q,
 \qquad
 \chi_v=-p,
 \qquad
 (\dot\lambda,\dot\zeta^p)=\dot\lambda(1,\beta).
 \label{eq:finite_generalized_normality}
\end{equation}
Projection to \((p,q)\) gives \eqref{eq:finite_loading_conditions}, while the
rate direction retains \(\beta\).  This is the finite-deformation statement of
the apparent non-associativity.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The split \(\mbf F=\mbf A\bar{\mbf F}\) assigns distinct physical roles to
two plastic mechanisms.  True-plastic flow rearranges the material at fixed
true-plastic volume, while plastic distension changes the volume accommodated
by the material.  Their conjugate powers follow from the free-energy
inequality and therefore determine which closures are admissible.

Two levels of result should be kept separate.  An independent product elastic
domain provides conventional multi-surface associated plasticity.  Its total
observable rate can be non-normal to one projected surface, but the codimension
and multiplier arguments in Section~\ref{sec:two_mechanisms} preclude a generic
claim of exact equivalence to an arbitrary one-surface law.  The exact
Drucker--Prager reduction uses a stronger construction: a shared activation
rate, a dilatancy constraint, and stress-dependent dissipation.  These
assumptions supply the missing multiplier relation.

The decomposition also clarifies the dissipation carried by a dilating
frictional solid.  Plastic distension performs negative power during dilation
under compression.  The true-plastic mechanism supplies enough positive power
that their sum is \(\mu p\dot\gamma\).  Separate nonnegativity of both powers is
therefore appropriate for independently dissipative compaction, while
frictional dilation requires the coupled inequality.

The present derivation is local and constitutive.  A complete computational
model additionally requires an elastic energy, hardening or softening laws,
return mapping, and treatment of reversal and apex loading.  Those additions
must preserve the generalized power in \eqref{eq:plastic_dissipation}.  The
finite-deformation formulation provides the stress measures and internal-rate
updates needed for that development.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We developed a single-phase plasticity theory from the finite-deformation
split \(\mbf F=\mbf A\bar{\mbf F}\).  The free-energy inequality identifies
true-plastic deformation and plastic distension as distinct mechanisms
conjugate to a deviatoric Mandel-type stress and compression-positive mean
stress.  Independent yield mechanisms are associated on their product elastic
domain, but their projection alone does not reproduce a general classical
non-associated model.

An exact Drucker--Prager specialization follows from a coupled generalized
dissipation potential.  The construction gives the yield slope
\(M=\mu+\beta\), the dilation slope \(\beta\), and the nonnegative power
\(\mu p\dot\gamma\).  Normality holds in the generalized-force space of the
two internal mechanisms, while the reduced true-stress description has the
plastic potential \(g=q-\beta p\).  The same force--rate structure carries to
finite deformation through the true-plastic and distension factors.  In this
precise sense, the expanded kinematics resolves the apparent
non-associativity.
