\title{Apparent non-associated plasticity from distinct isochoric and
volumetric mechanisms}
\author{John T. Foster}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
	Frictional materials can change volume as they shear. Pressure-dependent
	plasticity models often use one function to decide when plastic flow starts
	and a second function to prescribe the volume change that accompanies it.
	We examine a framework in which plastic shape change and plastic volume
	change are distinct mechanisms. The finite-deformation kinematics follow
	from \(\mbf F=\mbf A\bar{\mbf F}\), where \(\mbf F\) is the deformation
	gradient, \(\mbf A\) is the distension gradient, and \(\bar{\mbf F}\) is the
	true-deformation gradient. An elastic--plastic split of each factor assigns
	the plastic shape change of the solid material to \(\bar{\mbf F}^{p}\) and
	the plastic change in the volume assigned to it to \(\mbf A^{p}\). Under
	the Flory closure, the plastic distension \(a^p\) is the conventional
	plastic volume ratio. A microstructural distension that stores its own
	energy requires an additional state variable, conjugate force, and
	evolution law.
	The isothermal free-energy inequality then shows which stress drives each
	plastic rate. With separate yield conditions, each mechanism obeys an
	associated flow rule. When both mechanisms flow, their combined rate is a
	positive combination of the outward normals to their yield surfaces, so it
	is an outward normal to the full elastic domain where the surfaces meet.
	The independent model remains associated.

	For an isotropic, cohesionless material under monotone compression, we use a
	stress-dependent dissipation potential and a dilation constraint that give
	both mechanisms one activation condition.  Its small-strain limit recovers
	the Drucker--Prager yield surface together with a distinct plastic
	potential. The dissipation sets the difference between the friction and
	dilation slopes. Under the same proportional compressive loading, the
	independent model stays at the stress point where both yield conditions are
	active and accommodates the imposed volume reduction by compaction. The
	coupled model instead moves along the Drucker--Prager surface while
	dilating. The construction carries to finite deformation under the Flory
	closure and preserves nonnegative plastic dissipation. A homogeneous
	finite-deformation solution verifies the stress path, plastic volume change,
	dissipation, and small-strain limit. In the coupled model, the plastic rate
	is normal to the yield surface in generalized-force space when the pressure
	is held fixed. The pressure dependence of the dissipative strength and the
	dilation constraint then make the flow rule non-associated in the physical
	stresses.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Frictional materials change volume as they shear. Plasticity models for these
materials commonly name one surface that activates plastic loading and a
separate potential that sets the plastic flow direction.  This split permits
independent calibration of friction and dilation angles, but the resulting flow
rule is not normal to a single yield surface in the usual stress variables.
Classical associated plasticity instead takes
the plastic rate to be normal to the yield surface.  Drucker (1950) derived this
structure from work-hardening and ideal-plasticity assumptions, and Bland (1957)
showed its equivalence to a plastic potential identical to the yield function
\cite{drucker1950,bland1957}.  Conventional frictional models therefore use one
function to activate plastic loading and another to prescribe the observed
dilation rate, whereas classical associated plasticity requires a single
function to serve both roles.

Drucker and Prager (1952) introduced the pressure-dependent yield condition that
we use as the cohesionless reference model \cite{druckerprager1952}.  At finite strain,
the multiplicative elastic--plastic split and its intermediate configuration
are established modeling choices \cite{lee1969}, and maximum-plastic-dissipation
formulations already connect that split, the Mandel stress, and associated flow
\cite{simo1988}.  Finite-strain pressure-sensitive models likewise combine
Mandel-type stresses with inelastic volume change and cap-type response
\cite{bennett2019}.  The distension factorization below must therefore be read
against this established separation of volumetric and isochoric response. Where
the microscopic mechanism is crystalline slip, Reina and Conti (2014) derive
the split from the kinematics of slip in a two-dimensional construction
\cite[Secs.~4 and 6]{reina2014}. For granular rearrangement and pore collapse,
the factors remain constitutive assumptions, and their physical adequacy
requires material-specific evidence.

To account for plastic work, a thermodynamic formulation pairs each internal
rate with the force that acts through it.  Collins and Houlsby (1997) showed that an associated flow
rule written in this larger set of forces can become non-associated once the
internal forces are expressed through the usual stresses
\cite[Sec.~5]{collins1997}.  Within that setting they also gave a
stress-dependent dissipation construction for Drucker--Prager plasticity
\cite[Sec.~5]{collins1997}.  Other convex, energy-based dissipation models
derive pressure-dependent non-associated flow in the same spirit
\cite{srinivasa2010,ulloa2021}.  Separately, Drumheller's (2000) mixture kinematics
split the deformation into true deformation and distension, with elastic--plastic
factors and a distinct plastic power for each mechanism
\cite[Secs.~3 and 8]{drumheller2000}.  The two constructions fit together: the
shear rate of the Collins--Houlsby model becomes the isochoric plastic rate
that changes true deformation, and its volumetric rate becomes the plastic
distension.

We use Drumheller's terminology to give the rates in the Collins--Houlsby
construction a finite-deformation interpretation.  For a single solid phase,
the split \(\mbf F=\mbf A\bar{\mbf F}\) gives the isochoric plastic
deformation and the plastic distension separate elastic--plastic factors and
separate power-conjugate forces.  We consider two ways of combining them.  In
the independent model, each mechanism has its own yield condition and plastic
multiplier.  In the coupled model, a dilatancy constraint and a stress-dependent
dissipation potential give both mechanisms one multiplier.
This coupling produces the Drucker--Prager yield surface and, with it, a
different plastic-potential slope.  For the closed finite-deformation model,
the Flory constraint makes the resulting multiplicative split algebraically
equivalent to the classical volumetric--isochoric split.  At small strain, the
dissipation construction is that of Collins and Houlsby.

The independent model remains associated with its full elastic domain. In the
coupled model, the flow direction is normal to the yield surface in
generalized-force space at fixed pressure, but non-associated in physical
stress space. We also identify the stored energies that each model allows and
give an exact homogeneous finite-deformation example.

Section~\ref{sec:kinematics} defines the finite-deformation split and the
closure that assigns deformation to its factors, and
Section~\ref{sec:thermodynamics} derives the conjugate forces and plastic
power.  Section~\ref{sec:two_mechanisms} examines the associated
two-mechanism theory and its combined plastic strain rate.
Section~\ref{sec:drucker_prager}
constructs the small-strain pressure-dependent specialization exactly, and
Section~\ref{sec:finite_lift} extends it to finite deformation.

\section{Single-phase kinematics}
\label{sec:kinematics}

Let \(\bs\varphi(\mbf X,t)\) map a material point \(\mbf X\) in the reference
configuration to its current position \(\mbf x\) at time \(t\). The deformation gradient
and its Jacobian are
\begin{equation*}
	\mbf F=\frac{\partial\bs\varphi}{\partial\mbf X},
	\qquad
	J=\det\mbf F>0.
\end{equation*}
A porous body can change volume through compression of its solid material
and through changes in the space between solid regions. Drumheller's (2000)
mixture kinematics distinguish these changes through true deformation and
distension \cite[Secs.~3--4]{drumheller2000}. We adopt that distinction for a
single solid phase and write
\begin{equation}
	\mbf F=\mbf A\bar{\mbf F},
	\qquad
	J=a\bar J,
	\qquad
	a=\det\mbf A,
	\qquad
	\bar J=\det\bar{\mbf F}.
	\label{eq:distension_split}
\end{equation}
This factorization follows Drumheller's true-deformation and distension
construction \cite[Secs.~3 and 8.2]{drumheller2000}. The two tensors describe
successive local maps. First, the true-deformation tensor \(\bar{\mbf F}\) maps
a reference material line element into an intermediate configuration that
represents deformation of the solid material. The distension tensor \(\mbf A\)
then maps that element from the intermediate configuration into the current
spatial tangent space and accounts for the volume assigned to the material.
The determinants \(a\) and \(\bar J\) therefore separate the two volumetric
changes. Assigning a deformation to the two factors requires the closure
developed below. The factors are local two-point maps, so global compatibility
is an additional requirement.

The scalar quantity \(a\) has an established physical meaning in
porous-compaction models. Herrmann's (1969) pressure--distension, or
\(p\)--\(\alpha\), model distinguishes the bulk specific volume from the
specific volume of the solid material \cite{herrmann1969}. Their ratio measures
the extra volume associated with pores. Drumheller connects this classical
scalar distension to the determinant of a distension gradient
\cite[Sec.~4]{drumheller2000}. That connection motivates the volumetric
interpretation of \(\mbf A\); its full tensorial form still requires a choice
such as the isotropic restriction used here.

Either factor can change elastically or plastically. Superscripts \(e\) and
\(p\) denote elastic and plastic factors, respectively, in the splits
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:plastic_splits}
	\begin{align}
		\mbf A       & =\mbf A^e\mbf A^p,
		\label{eq:distension_plastic_split}               \\
		\bar{\mbf F} & =\bar{\mbf F}^{e}\bar{\mbf F}^{p},
		\qquad
		\det\bar{\mbf F}^{p}=1.
		\label{eq:true_plastic_split}
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
The isochoric condition assigns all plastic volume change to \(\mbf A^p\).
Drumheller's (2000) finite-deformation construction makes the same distinction: the
plastic factor of true deformation is incompressible, whereas the plastic-distension factor
may change volume
\cite[eqs.~(71)--(74)]{drumheller2000}.

The pressure-dependent specialization restricts both elastic and plastic
distension to be isotropic,
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{aligned}
		\mbf A & =a^{1/3}\mbf I,
		       & \mbf A^e        & =(a^e)^{1/3}\mbf I,
		       & \mbf A^p        & =(a^p)^{1/3}\mbf I, \\
		a^e    & =\det\mbf A^e,
		       & a^p             & =\det\mbf A^p,
		       & a               & =a^e a^p,           \\
		\zeta  & =\ln a,
		       & \zeta           & =\zeta^e+\zeta^p.
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Here \(\mbf I\) is the identity tensor, \(\zeta^e=\ln a^e\), and
\(\zeta^p=\ln a^p\). A dot denotes a material time derivative. The velocity
gradient then separates into a spherical distension rate and a
true-deformation rate,
\begin{equation}
	\mbf L=\dot{\mbf F}\mbf F^{-1}
	=\frac{\dot\zeta}{3}\mbf I
	+\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}\bar{\mbf F}^{-1}.
	\label{eq:velocity_gradient}
\end{equation}
The split of \(\bar{\mbf F}\) introduces a second intermediate configuration:
\(\bar{\mbf F}^{p}\) maps reference line elements into it, and
\(\bar{\mbf F}^{e}\) maps them onward to the intermediate configuration of
\eqref{eq:distension_split}. The isochoric plastic velocity gradient in this
plastic intermediate configuration is
\begin{equation*}
	\bar{\mbf L}^{p}
	=\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}^{p}(\bar{\mbf F}^{p})^{-1},
	\qquad
	\tr\bar{\mbf L}^{p}=0.
\end{equation*}
Differentiating \eqref{eq:true_plastic_split} separates the true-deformation
rate into its elastic and plastic parts,
\begin{equation}
	\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}\bar{\mbf F}^{-1}
	=\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}^{e}(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^{-1}
	+\bar{\mbf F}^{e}\bar{\mbf L}^{p}
	(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^{-1}.
	\label{eq:true_rate_split}
\end{equation}

The plastic part of this rate reaches the current configuration as
\(\bar{\mbf F}^e\bar{\mbf L}^p(\bar{\mbf F}^e)^{-1}\). It is therefore not the
difference between \(\mbf L\) and the elastic true-deformation rate: that
difference also retains the spherical distension rate \(\dot\zeta\mbf I/3\).
For this reason, \(\bar{\mbf L}^{p}\) is the isochoric plastic velocity
gradient in its intermediate configuration, not the plastic part of the
spatial velocity gradient.

\subsection{Closure of the kinematic split}
\label{sec:kinematic_closure}

The split in \eqref{eq:distension_split} requires a rule that assigns the
total deformation to its factors. For isotropic distension, \(J=a\bar J\) fixes
only the product of the distension and true-deformation Jacobians. The scalar
\(a\) must therefore come from an evolution equation or a kinematic
constraint. Once \(a\) is known, \(\mbf A=a^{1/3}\mbf I\) and
\(\bar{\mbf F}=a^{-1/3}\mbf F\) follow. Jacobi's determinant identity applied
to \(a=\det\mbf A\) gives the rate relation
\begin{equation*}
	\frac{\dot a}{a}
	=\tr\left(\dot{\mbf A}\mbf A^{-1}\right).
\end{equation*}

In Drumheller's (2000) mixture theory, constituent mass balance relates this
rate to the rates of volume fraction and mass exchange. Without mass exchange
and for unit reference volume fraction, the distension is the inverse of the
current volume fraction \cite[eq.~(36)]{drumheller2000}. If the factors are
instead normalized to the identity in a porous reference state, \(a\) measures
the ratio of the reference to current solid volume fractions. The difference
between an absolute bulk-to-solid volume ratio and its reference-normalized
change therefore matters when comparing with a \(p\)--\(\alpha\) model. Mass
balance supplies the required connection whenever volume fraction is retained
as a state variable, and a constitutive law then determines how that variable
evolves.

The single-phase model here takes the simpler route. A kinematic constraint,
introduced below, assigns the total volume change to distension, and the
closure then determines both factors. The flow rule
\eqref{eq:finite_distension_flow} supplies the plastic rate
\(\dot a^p/a^p\). An independent microstructural interpretation of distension
would instead need the energetic structure examined in
Section~\ref{sec:energy_class}.

We close the model by assigning the total volume change to distension and
the remaining shape change to true deformation. This is the standard Flory split
\cite{flory1961}.  Foster et al. (2026) obtain the same specialization from the
distension split for incompressible solid phases
\cite[Sec.~2, eq.~(17)]{foster2026reactingporous}:
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:flory_closure}
	\begin{align}
		\mbf A       & =J^{1/3}\mbf I,
		             & a                & =J,
		\label{eq:flory_distension}           \\
		\bar{\mbf F} & =J^{-1/3}\mbf F,
		             & \bar J           & =1.
		\label{eq:flory_true_deformation}
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
This choice determines both factors directly from \(\mbf F\).  Together with
\eqref{eq:plastic_splits}, it also gives
\(\det\bar{\mbf F}^{e}=\det\bar{\mbf F}^{p}=1\).  Under this closure, \(a^p\)
is the conventional plastic volume ratio.  The deviatoric and volumetric
plastic rates remain distinct, and each keeps its own contribution to plastic
power.

The closed case also makes contact with conventional multiplicative
plasticity.  Because the spherical factors commute with the true-deformation
factors we can define
\begin{equation*}
	\mbf F^e=(a^e)^{1/3}\bar{\mbf F}^{e},
	\qquad
	\mbf F^p=(a^p)^{1/3}\bar{\mbf F}^{p},
	\qquad
	\mbf F=\mbf F^e\mbf F^p,
	\qquad
	\det\mbf F^p=a^p.
\end{equation*}
The Flory-closed kinematics therefore identify the conventional isochoric and
volumetric plastic factors with isochoric plastic deformation and plastic
distension. A porosity or volume-fraction model would require both a
microstructural closure and compatible energetic forces.
Section~\ref{sec:energy_class} derives that energetic requirement.

\section{Free-energy restriction and plastic power}
\label{sec:thermodynamics}

Let \(\psi\) denote the isothermal Helmholtz free energy per unit reference
volume.  The energy depends on the two elastic factors and on one hardening
variable for each mechanism, \(\kappa_d\) for isochoric plastic deformation and
\(\kappa_v\) for distension.  The model therefore takes
\begin{equation*}
	\psi=\psi
	\left(
	\bar{\mbf F}^{e},\zeta^e,\kappa_d,\kappa_v
	\right).
\end{equation*}
The Kirchhoff stress is \(\bs\tau=\mbf P\mbf F^T=J\bs\sigma\),
where \(\mbf P\) and \(\bs\sigma\) are the first Piola and Cauchy
stresses, respectively.
Material frame indifference requires the free energy to remain unchanged when
\(\bar{\mbf F}^{e}\) is subjected to a rigid rotation, and angular-momentum balance
makes \(\bs\tau\) symmetric.  The pressure-dependent specialization below
further assumes an isotropic elastic response, for which the Mandel-type
stress is symmetric.  The local free-energy inequality is
\begin{equation*}
	\mathcal D
	=\mbf P:\dot{\mbf F}-\dot\psi
	\ge 0,
\end{equation*}
where \(\mathcal D\) is power per unit reference volume, and
\eqref{eq:velocity_gradient} and \eqref{eq:true_rate_split} separate the
mechanical power into distension, elastic true-deformation, and isochoric
plastic contributions,
\begin{equation*}
	\mbf P:\dot{\mbf F}
	=\left[\bs\tau(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^{-T}\right]:\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}^{e}
	+\left[(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^T\bs\tau(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^{-T}\right]:\bar{\mbf L}^{p}
	+\frac{1}{3}\tr(\bs\tau)\dot\zeta.
\end{equation*}
The corresponding free-energy rate is
\begin{equation*}
	\dot\psi=
	\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bar{\mbf F}^{e}}
	:\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}^{e}
	+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\zeta^e}
	\left(\dot\zeta-\dot\zeta^p\right)
	+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\kappa_d}\dot\kappa_d
	+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\kappa_v}\dot\kappa_v.
\end{equation*}
Substitution of the mechanical power and free-energy rate gives the collected
free-energy inequality,
\begin{align}
	\mathcal D={} &
	\left[
		\bs\tau(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^{-T}
		-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bar{\mbf F}^{e}}
		\right]:\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}^{e}
	+\left[
		\frac{1}{3}\tr\bs\tau
		-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\zeta^e}
		\right]\dot\zeta
	\notag                  \\
	              & +\left[
		(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^T\bs\tau(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^{-T}
		\right]:\bar{\mbf L}^{p}
	+\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\zeta^e}\dot\zeta^p
	-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\kappa_d}\dot\kappa_d
	-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\kappa_v}\dot\kappa_v
	\ge0.
	\label{eq:collected_free_energy_inequality}
\end{align}
For the general distension split, we first take \(a\) as an independent
kinematic variable.  Its scalar rate and the elastic true-deformation rate can
therefore vary independently. Their coefficients
in \eqref{eq:collected_free_energy_inequality} must vanish, giving the
reversible restrictions
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:reversible_restrictions}
	\begin{align}
		\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bar{\mbf F}^{e}}
		 & =\bs\tau(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^{-T},
		\label{eq:true_stress_restriction}  \\
		\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\zeta^e}
		 & =\frac{1}{3}\tr\bs\tau.
		\label{eq:distension_stress_restriction}
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
\subsection{Admissible energy class and closure}
\label{sec:energy_class}

We first ask whether the two factors can store energy independently. If their
elastic rates can vary independently and no other microstructural power enters
the balance, both relations in \eqref{eq:reversible_restrictions} must hold.
Contracting the first with \(\bar{\mbf F}^e\) and using the second gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bar{\mbf F}^e}:\bar{\mbf F}^e
 =3\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\zeta^e}.
 \label{eq:energy_scaling_identity}
\end{equation}
For any scalar \(c\), increasing one factor while decreasing the other by
the corresponding amount leaves the energy unchanged:
\begin{align}
 \frac{d}{dc}\psi\left(e^c\bar{\mbf F}^e,\zeta^e-3c,\kappa_d,\kappa_v\right)
 &=0, \label{eq:energy_scaling_invariance}\\
 \psi\left(\bar{\mbf F}^e,\zeta^e,\kappa_d,\kappa_v\right)
 &=\widehat\psi\left(e^{\zeta^e/3}\bar{\mbf F}^e,\kappa_d,\kappa_v\right).
 \label{eq:energy_class}
\end{align}
The second statement follows by taking \(c=\zeta^e/3\) in the first;
\(\widehat\psi\) is the same energy expressed through the combined elastic
factor. Compensating rescaling of the two factors therefore leaves the energy
unchanged. An independently energetic distension cannot be obtained from this
power balance: it requires additional terms in the balance and the conjugate
forces those terms imply. A physical porosity, for example, needs its own state
variable, thermodynamic force, and balance or evolution law, and that force
must carry the stored microstructural energy in addition to pressure work.

Under the Flory closure \eqref{eq:flory_closure}, elastic volume change is
carried by distension. Variations of the elastic true-deformation factor must
preserve volume and therefore satisfy
\(\tr[\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}^e(\bar{\mbf F}^e)^{-1}]=0\).  With \(\dev\) denoting
the deviatoric part, the resulting restriction on the stress response is
\begin{equation}
	\dev\left[
		\left(
		\bs\tau(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^{-T}
		-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\bar{\mbf F}^{e}}
		\right)(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^T
		\right]=\mbf0.
 \label{eq:flory_projected_restriction}
\end{equation}
This projected condition is the alternative to imposing the full tensor
relation. It is all that the admissible variations require: if the energy is
also defined for elastic true deformations that change volume, its derivative
at the permitted isochoric states need satisfy only this deviatoric condition.
Imposing the full stress relation \eqref{eq:true_stress_restriction} instead
makes the bracket vanish, so the deviatoric condition then holds
automatically. Equation~\eqref{eq:distension_stress_restriction} determines the
spherical response in this constrained derivation. We use these conditions for
the closed finite-deformation model. The remaining plastic power is unchanged
because \(\bar{\mbf L}^{p}\) is traceless.

Throughout the finite-deformation formulas, \(p\) is the compression-positive
Kirchhoff pressure. The corresponding Cauchy pressure is
\(-\tr\bs\sigma/3=p/J\). Plastic dissipation
\(\mathcal D^p\) is power per reference volume; division by \(J\) gives the rate
per current volume. Both rates have units of stress per time.
The compression-positive mean Kirchhoff stress, the deviatoric stress that
drives isochoric plastic flow, and the hardening forces are
\begin{align*}
	p      & =-\frac{1}{3}\tr\bs\tau,
	\\
	\mbf M & =\dev\left[
		(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^T\bs\tau
		(\bar{\mbf F}^{e})^{-T}
		\right],
	\\
	Q_d    & =-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\kappa_d},
	\qquad
	Q_v=-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\kappa_v}.
\end{align*}
Substituting \eqref{eq:reversible_restrictions} into
\eqref{eq:collected_free_energy_inequality} leaves the plastic dissipation,
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal D^p
	=\mbf M:\bar{\mbf L}^{p}
	-p\dot\zeta^p
	+Q_d\dot\kappa_d+Q_v\dot\kappa_v
	\ge0.
	\label{eq:plastic_dissipation}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:plastic_dissipation} shows how each internal mechanism
contributes to plastic power.  The deviatoric stress \(\mbf M\) is conjugate
to the isochoric rate \(\bar{\mbf L}^{p}\), whereas \(-p\) is conjugate to the
logarithmic plastic-distension rate
\(\dot\zeta^p=\dot a^p/a^p\).  These powers are the single-phase counterparts
of the plastic true-deformation and plastic-distension powers in Drumheller's constitutive
theory
\cite[eqs.~(108)--(112)]{drumheller2000}.

\section{Independent mechanisms and their combined response}
\label{sec:two_mechanisms}

We first allow shape change and volume change to start and evolve
independently. For the isochoric mechanism, the equivalent stress \(q\) and the
equivalent plastic strain rate \(\dot\gamma\) are, using the Frobenius norm
\(\lVert\cdot\rVert\),
\begin{equation}
	q=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\lVert\mbf M\rVert,
	\qquad
	\dot\gamma=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}
	\lVert\bar{\mbf L}^{p}\rVert.
	\label{eq:equivalent_quantities}
\end{equation}
We consider isochoric plastic flow for \(q>0\), where the normal is unique:
\begin{equation}
	\mbf N=\frac{3}{2}\frac{\mbf M}{q},
	\qquad
	\bar{\mbf L}^{p}=\dot\gamma\mbf N,
	\qquad
	\mbf M:\bar{\mbf L}^{p}=q\dot\gamma.
	\label{eq:deviatoric_normal}
\end{equation}
The tensor \(\mbf N\) is the outward normal to a level surface of \(q\), and
its normalization makes \(\dot\gamma\) agree with
\eqref{eq:equivalent_quantities}.  The isotropic specialization takes
\(\mbf N\) to be symmetric, which sets the plastic spin to zero in the
intermediate configuration.

Isochoric plastic deformation and plastic distension may yield
independently.  The elastic range is defined by the two conditions
\begin{align*}
	f_d(q,\kappa_d)  & =q-Y(\kappa_d)\le0,
	\\
	f_v(-p,\kappa_v) & =|p|-K(\kappa_v)\le0,
\end{align*}
where \(Y\ge0\) is the yield strength for isochoric plastic deformation and
\(K\ge0\) is the yield strength for plastic distension.  On smooth portions of
these surfaces, the model postulates associated flow rules and hardening laws
for the two mechanisms,
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:product_flow_rules}
	\begin{align}
		\bar{\mbf L}^{p}
		 & =\dot\lambda_d\frac{\partial f_d}{\partial\mbf M},
		\qquad
		\dot\kappa_d=\dot\lambda_d h_d,
		\label{eq:true_associated_flow}                       \\
		\dot\zeta^p
		 & =\dot\lambda_v\frac{\partial f_v}{\partial(-p)},
		\qquad
		\dot\kappa_v=\dot\lambda_v h_v,
		\label{eq:distension_associated_flow}
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where \(\dot\lambda_d\) and \(\dot\lambda_v\) are plastic multiplier rates,
and \(h_d,h_v\ge0\) determine the corresponding hardening rates. Each plastic
multiplier rate satisfies the Karush--Kuhn--Tucker
conditions
\begin{equation*}
	\dot\lambda_i\ge0,
	\qquad
	f_i\le0,
	\qquad
	\dot\lambda_i f_i=0,
	\qquad
	i\in\{d,v\}.
\end{equation*}
During plastic loading, the stress must remain on the yield surface:
\begin{equation}
	\dot f_i=0
	\quad\text{when}\quad
	\dot\lambda_i>0,
	\qquad
	i\in\{d,v\}.
	\label{eq:consistency}
\end{equation}
Together with the stress update and the hardening laws in
\eqref{eq:product_flow_rules}, these conditions determine the plastic
multipliers during loading.

Energetic hardening contributes directly to the dissipation inequality.  The
active conditions and \eqref{eq:product_flow_rules} give
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:dissipative_hardening_specialization}
	\begin{gather}
		\mathcal D^p
		=\dot\lambda_d\left(Y+Q_dh_d\right)
		+\dot\lambda_v\left(K+Q_vh_v\right)\ge0,
		\label{eq:hardening_dissipation}                    \\
		Y+Q_dh_d
		\ge0,
		\label{eq:true_hardening_restriction}               \\
		K+Q_vh_v
		\ge0.
		\label{eq:distension_hardening_restriction}
	\end{gather}
\end{subequations}
The restrictions \eqref{eq:true_hardening_restriction} and
\eqref{eq:distension_hardening_restriction} are sufficient for
\eqref{eq:hardening_dissipation} because the mechanisms activate independently.
For purely dissipative hardening, we take
\(Q_d=Q_v=0\) whenever \(h_d,h_v>0\), so the hardening variables change the
yield strengths without storing energy.  The dissipation then reduces to
\begin{equation*}
	\mathcal D^p
	=Y\dot\lambda_d+K\dot\lambda_v
	\ge0.
\end{equation*}
Hardening can also store energy, provided that
\eqref{eq:true_hardening_restriction} and
\eqref{eq:distension_hardening_restriction} hold.

When both mechanisms flow, each contributes its own normal direction. Viewed
through the total plastic strain rate, their combined flow can appear
non-associated with either individual yield surface, although it remains
associated with the complete elastic domain. At small strain,
\eqref{eq:velocity_gradient} separates this rate into a traceless isochoric
plastic part and a spherical plastic-distension part.  Let
\(\dot{\bs\varepsilon}^{p}_{d}\) denote the former.  Since \(\dot\zeta^p\) is
the plastic volumetric strain rate, its spherical contribution is
\(\dot\zeta^p\mbf I/3\), and hence
\begin{equation*}
	\dot{\bs\varepsilon}^{p}
	=\dot{\bs\varepsilon}^{p}_{d}
	+\frac{1}{3}\dot\zeta^p\mbf I.
\end{equation*}
When both mechanisms are active, the associated flow rules
\eqref{eq:product_flow_rules} give
\begin{equation*}
	\dot{\bs\varepsilon}^{p}
	=\dot\lambda_d\frac{\partial f_d}{\partial\mbf M}
	+\frac{\dot\lambda_v}{3}
	\frac{\partial f_v}{\partial(-p)}\mbf I.
\end{equation*}
The second term adds volume change to the isochoric flow. This added part does
not point normal to the shear yield surface \(f_d=0\). At a fixed hardening
state, however, the elastic domain is the intersection \(f_d\le0\) and
\(f_v\le0\). The combined plastic rate is a nonnegative sum of the outward
normals to the yield surfaces that are active, and such sums are outward
normals of the complete domain, including at a corner. Together with the zero
rate they form the normal cone of that domain, so the combined flow is
associated with the complete domain. The apparent non-association with either
individual surface is therefore different from non-association relative to a
single physical-stress yield surface.

Two independent yield conditions generally cannot be replaced by one
non-associated yield surface.  When both mechanisms are active,
\(f_d=0\) and \(f_v=0\) impose two equations, whereas a regular one-surface
model imposes only one.  In addition,
\eqref{eq:consistency} determines \(\dot\lambda_d\) and \(\dot\lambda_v\)
through separate consistency equations, and those equations do not generally
fix the multiplier ratio that a chosen plastic potential would require.  Exact
equivalence therefore requires a shared activation condition or an explicit
constitutive coupling.

The loading cases show this difference without specifying an elastic law.  At
\(f_d=0\) and \(f_v<0\), the independent model permits isochoric plastic flow
with \(\dot\zeta^p=0\); at \(f_v=0\) and \(f_d<0\), it permits purely
volumetric plastic flow.  Where the surfaces meet, the two consistency
equations determine separate multipliers whose ratio is not fixed in advance.
In the coupled model below, the mechanisms share one yield surface and the
fixed ratio \(\dot\zeta^p/\dot\gamma=\beta\), where \(\beta\) is the dilation
slope. Coupling therefore fixes how much volume change accompanies a given
amount of isochoric plastic flow.

The energy balance also constrains how the mechanisms can combine. Under
compression \(p>0\), dilation has \(\dot\zeta^p>0\), so its contribution
\(-p\dot\zeta^p\) in \eqref{eq:plastic_dissipation} is negative.  If the
distension term alone had to be nonnegative, plastic distension could compact
the material under compression but could not dilate it.  A dilating frictional
material instead draws on part of the positive isochoric plastic power to pay
for the volume increase.

\section{Coupled mechanisms and Drucker--Prager plasticity}
\label{sec:drucker_prager}

We now connect the two mechanisms to the familiar small-strain
Drucker--Prager model. At small strain, the finite-deformation quantities
reduce to
\begin{equation}
	\bs\tau=J\bs\sigma\longrightarrow\bs\sigma,
	\qquad
	\mbf M\longrightarrow\dev\bs\sigma,
	\qquad
	\operatorname{sym}\bar{\mbf L}^{p}
	\longrightarrow\dot{\bs\varepsilon}^{p}_{d},
	\qquad
	\dot\zeta^p\longrightarrow\tr\dot{\bs\varepsilon}^{p}.
	\label{eq:small_strain_limit}
\end{equation}
For aligned flow with \(q>0\), equation~\eqref{eq:deviatoric_normal} gives
\(\mbf M:\bar{\mbf L}^p=q\dot\gamma\).  Neglecting energetic hardening, the
limits in \eqref{eq:small_strain_limit} therefore reduce
\eqref{eq:plastic_dissipation} to the combined plastic power
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal D^p=q\dot\gamma-p\dot\zeta^p.
	\label{eq:small_strain_power}
\end{equation}
We take compression as positive and restrict the response to \(p\ge0\) and
\(\dot\gamma\ge0\). Plastic dilation is prescribed in proportion to plastic
shear,
\begin{equation*}
	\dot\zeta^p=\beta\dot\gamma,
	\qquad
	\beta\ge0.
\end{equation*}
This proportional rate relation is an imposed constitutive constraint that
couples the two plastic rates. The dissipation potential below specifies both
the admissible rate pairs and the power they dissipate:
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal R(p;\dot\gamma,\dot\zeta^p)
	=
	\begin{cases}
		\mu p\dot\gamma,
		         & \dot\gamma\ge0\ \text{and}\
		\dot\zeta^p=\beta\dot\gamma,           \\
		+\infty, & \text{otherwise},
	\end{cases}
	\qquad \mu\ge0,
	\label{eq:dissipation_potential}
\end{equation}
Here \(\mu\) is a dimensionless resistance coefficient, and \(\mu p\) is the
dissipative strength at pressure \(p\). For each fixed \(p\ge0\), the potential
is convex and positively homogeneous in the rates. It permits zero plastic flow
and assigns infinite cost to rate pairs that violate the dilation rule.
This construction adapts the
stress-dependent dissipation and dilation constraint used by Collins and
Houlsby (1997) in their Drucker--Prager example
\cite[eqs.~(5.8)--(5.14)]{collins1997}.

To find the range of forces that produces no plastic flow, we hold the
pressure \(p\) fixed. Let \(\chi_q\) and \(\chi_v\)
denote the generalized thermodynamic forces conjugate to \(\dot\gamma\) and
\(\dot\zeta^p\), respectively.  Because the conjugate rates are strain-like,
these scalar forces have units of stress, and they are the reduced
counterparts of the generalized stresses used by Collins and Houlsby (1997)
\cite[eqs.~(2.10)--(2.11) and (5.11)--(5.13)]{collins1997}.
At zero plastic rate, admissible forces cannot perform more work through any
rate pair than the dissipation potential assigns to that pair.  Therefore,
\begin{align}
	\chi_q\dot\gamma+\chi_v\dot\zeta^p
	 & \le\mathcal R(p;\dot\gamma,\dot\zeta^p)
	\quad\text{for every rate pair}
	\notag                                     \\
	 & \iff
	\chi_q+\beta\chi_v-\mu p\le0.
	\label{eq:zero_rate_force_condition}
\end{align}
Thus, for each fixed value of \(p\), the admissible driving forces satisfy
\begin{equation}
	f_g(\chi_q,\chi_v;p)
	=\chi_q+\beta\chi_v-\mu p
	\le0.
	\label{eq:generalized_yield}
\end{equation}
On the boundary of this region, associated flow gives the plastic rates and
loading conditions,
\begin{equation}
	\begin{gathered}
		(\dot\gamma,\dot\zeta^p)
		=\dot\lambda\frac{\partial f_g}{\partial(\chi_q,\chi_v)}
		=\dot\lambda(1,\beta), \\
		\dot\lambda\ge0,
		\qquad
		f_g\le0,
		\qquad
		\dot\lambda f_g=0,
		\qquad
		\dot f_g=0\ \text{when}\ \dot\lambda>0.
	\end{gathered}
	\label{eq:generalized_loading_conditions}
\end{equation}
The scalar \(\dot\lambda\) is the common plastic multiplier rate for the
coupled mechanisms.
At fixed pressure, the coupled rate is normal to this boundary in the
generalized-force space \((\chi_q,\chi_v)\). During plastic loading the
pressure can change, and the forces must remain on the corresponding yield
boundary. The condition \(\dot f_g=0\) therefore accounts for changes in both
the forces and the pressure. As shown below, the resulting flow direction need
not be normal to the yield surface expressed in physical stresses.

For the cohesionless model without kinematic hardening, the conjugate forces
coincide with those in the physical power \eqref{eq:small_strain_power},
\begin{equation}
	\chi_q=q,
	\qquad
	\chi_v=-p.
	\label{eq:true_force_identification}
\end{equation}
Substitution into \eqref{eq:generalized_yield} gives the yield condition in
terms of pressure and equivalent shear stress,
\begin{equation*}
	f(p,q)=q-Mp\le0,
	\qquad
	M=\mu+\beta.
\end{equation*}
After this substitution the yield surface has volumetric slope
\(M=\mu+\beta\), whereas the plastic rate in
\eqref{eq:generalized_loading_conditions} has volumetric slope \(\beta\).
Written directly in terms of pressure and equivalent shear stress, the plastic
rate therefore follows from the potential
\begin{equation}
	g(p,q)=q-\beta p,
	\qquad
	\dot{\bs\varepsilon}^{p}
	=\dot\lambda\frac{\partial g}{\partial\bs\sigma}.
	\label{eq:plastic_potential}
\end{equation}
Here the pressure, deviatoric stress, and equivalent shear stress are
\begin{equation*}
	p=-\frac{1}{3}\tr\bs\sigma,
	\qquad
	\mbf s=\dev\bs\sigma,
	\qquad
	q=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\lVert\mbf s\rVert.
\end{equation*}
For \(q>0\), substitution into \eqref{eq:plastic_potential} gives
\begin{equation*}
	\dot{\bs\varepsilon}^{p}
	=\dot\lambda\left(
	\frac{3}{2}\frac{\mbf s}{q}
	+\frac{\beta}{3}\mbf I
	\right),
	\qquad
	\dot\gamma=\dot\lambda,
	\qquad
	\tr\dot{\bs\varepsilon}^{p}=\beta\dot\lambda.
\end{equation*}
Normality to \(f=0\) would give the volumetric coefficient \(M\), whereas the
actual coefficient is \(\beta\).  Because \(M=\mu+\beta\), the flow is
non-associated whenever \(\mu>0\). Increasing the pressure increases the
dissipative strength \(\mu p\), which adds \(\mu\) to the slope of the
physical yield surface. The flow direction in generalized-force space is found
at fixed pressure, so it does not contain that change in strength. Its
volumetric coefficient is therefore \(\beta\).

The dissipation now follows directly from the active surface and the
dilatancy constraint,
\begin{align*}
	\mathcal D^p
	 & =q\dot\gamma-p\dot\zeta^p
	                              \\
	 & =\left(M-\beta\right)p\dot\gamma
	                              \\
	 & =\mu p\dot\gamma
	\ge0.
\end{align*}
Thus the frictional strength separates into the dilation slope \(\beta\) and
the part \(\mu\) that produces dissipation.  For \(\mu=0\), the flow is
associated and this idealized rate-independent mechanism dissipates no power.
Collins and Houlsby (1997) obtain the same separation in their stress-dependent
dissipation construction \cite[eq.~(5.14)]{collins1997}.

The exact equivalence applies to an isotropic material when the isochoric
plastic rate remains aligned with its driving stress, loading is monotone with
\(p\ge0\) and \(\dot\gamma\ge0\), \(\mu\) and \(\beta\) are constant, and
cohesion and back stress are zero.  Cohesion and hardening can be introduced by
replacing \(\mu p\) with a nonnegative state-dependent strength and prescribing
its evolution.  Changes in loading direction, corners, cyclic reversal, and
anisotropy require a rate potential that specifies the tensor direction and
rules that determine which yield condition is active.  These requirements are
what distinguish the coupled model from the model with separate yield
conditions in Section~\ref{sec:two_mechanisms}.



\subsection{Relation to prior dissipation formulations}
\label{sec:prior_dissipation}

Collins and Houlsby (1997) derived non-associated Drucker--Prager flow from
a dissipation principle. They impose the same linear dilation
constraint and stress-dependent dissipation used in
\eqref{eq:dissipation_potential} and obtain the same separation
\(M=\mu+\beta\) \cite[Sec.~5]{collins1997}.  Equations
\eqref{eq:zero_rate_force_condition}--\eqref{eq:true_force_identification}
state the admissible forces at zero plastic rate and then replace the
volumetric driving force by minus the pressure. This gives the same
small-strain constitutive law, written so that the power carried by each
mechanism is explicit.

The term \emph{coupled mechanisms} used here refers to a common activation
condition and plastic multiplier for the isochoric and volumetric mechanisms.
It is distinct from Collins and Houlsby's elastic--plastic coupling through the
thermodynamic potential \cite[Sec.~3]{collins1997}.

Srinivasa (2010) instead lets the dissipation function determine both the yield
surface and the non-associated flow direction, without imposing a separate
dilation constraint \cite{srinivasa2010}.  Ulloa et al. (2021) derive
non-associated flow from stored energy and a state-dependent dissipation
potential; their approach also includes hardening and more general
non-associated models \cite{ulloa2021}.  The present construction assigns the
shear and volume rates to distinct isochoric plastic and distension factors and
makes the closure requirement explicit. It compares independent activation
with a shared-multiplier response and carries that mechanism-resolved
interpretation to finite deformation.  Under the Flory closure, the two factors
are the conventional isochoric and volumetric plastic factors.

\subsection{Proportional loading comparison}
\label{sec:loading_comparison}

A small-strain proportional path shows how the two models respond.  Let the
elastic shear and bulk moduli be \(G>0\) and
\(K_e>0\), and impose the coaxial strain rate
\begin{equation*}
	\dot{\bs\varepsilon}
	=\dot\varepsilon_s\mbf N
	-\frac{\dot\varepsilon_v}{3}\mbf I,
	\qquad
	\dot\varepsilon_s>0,
	\qquad
	\dot\varepsilon_v>0,
\end{equation*}
where \(\dot\varepsilon_s\) is the equivalent deviatoric strain rate and
\(\dot\varepsilon_v=-\tr\dot{\bs\varepsilon}\) is the
compression-positive volumetric strain rate.  Take constant independent
strengths \(Y_0\) and \(K_0\), and start at their common corner
\(q=Y_0\), \(p=K_0\).  Choosing \(Y_0=MK_0\) places the coupled model at the
same stress point on its Drucker--Prager surface.

For the independent model, associated distension flow at \(p=K_0>0\) gives
\(\dot\zeta^p=-\dot\lambda_v\).  The elastic stress rates are
\begin{equation*}
	\dot q=3G(\dot\varepsilon_s-\dot\lambda_d),
	\qquad
	\dot p=K_e(\dot\varepsilon_v-\dot\lambda_v).
\end{equation*}
Consistency of both active surfaces therefore gives
\begin{equation*}
	\dot\lambda_d=\dot\varepsilon_s,
	\qquad
	\dot\lambda_v=\dot\varepsilon_v,
	\qquad
	\dot q=0,
	\qquad
	\dot p=0,
	\qquad
	\dot\zeta^p=-\dot\varepsilon_v<0.
\end{equation*}
The independent response remains at the corner: shear produces isochoric
plastic flow, while the imposed volume reduction produces plastic compaction.

For the coupled model, \(\dot\zeta^p=\beta\dot\lambda\), and the stress rates
under the same imposed path are
\begin{equation}
	\dot q=3G(\dot\varepsilon_s-\dot\lambda),
	\qquad
	\dot p=K_e(\dot\varepsilon_v+\beta\dot\lambda).
\label{eq:coupled_stress_rates}
\end{equation}
Consistency of \(q-Mp=0\) gives
\begin{equation}
	\dot\lambda
	=\frac{3G\dot\varepsilon_s-MK_e\dot\varepsilon_v}
	{3G+MK_e\beta}.
\label{eq:coupled_multiplier}
\end{equation}
For \(\beta>0\) and
\(3G\dot\varepsilon_s>MK_e\dot\varepsilon_v\), the multiplier is positive,
\(\dot\zeta^p=\beta\dot\lambda>0\), and
\(\dot q=M\dot p>0\).  Thus the coupled response moves along the
Drucker--Prager surface while dilating, whereas the independent response under
the same loading remains at its two-surface corner while compacting.

This comparison identifies the material behavior that each model represents.
The coupled model is appropriate when shear rearrangement causes plastic volume
expansion, as in the shear dilation of a dense frictional granular material.
The independent model is appropriate when volume change has its own activation
process, as in pore collapse or grain crushing.  A material that exhibits both
shear dilation and pressure-driven compaction would generally require a coupled
shear-dilation mechanism together with a separate compaction mechanism.

\section{Finite-deformation extension}
\label{sec:finite_lift}

We now extend the coupled model to finite deformation using the stresses and
rates that contribute to plastic power in
\eqref{eq:plastic_dissipation}.  This section adopts
the Flory closure \eqref{eq:flory_closure}, so \(a=J\),
\(\bar J=1\), and \(a^e=J/a^p\) follow from the imposed deformation
and the plastic state.  The specialization neglects energetic hardening.
Active flow is restricted to \(q>0\), where the normal is unique:
\begin{equation}
	q=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}\lVert\mbf M\rVert,
	\qquad
	\mbf N=\frac{3}{2}\frac{\mbf M}{q}.
	\label{eq:finite_equivalent_stress}
\end{equation}
Because this normal is divided by \(q\), plastic loading must begin from a
state with \(q>0\). The stress-free state has \(q=0\), so an elastic preload is
needed before the flow direction can be evaluated. A hydrostatic elastic
compression first enters \(p>0\), \(q=0\), \(f<0\); elastic shear then brings
the stress to the smooth surface at \(q=Mp>0\). The worked example below
follows this elastic-preload route. Plastic loading directly from the apex
requires a separate nonsmooth treatment.

The two internal mechanisms then evolve according to
\begin{subequations}
	\label{eq:finite_flow_rules}
	\begin{align}
		\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}^{p}(\bar{\mbf F}^{p})^{-1}
		 & =\dot\lambda\mbf N,
		\label{eq:finite_true_flow}      \\
		\dot\zeta^p
		 & =\frac{\dot a^p}{a^p}, \notag \\
		\dot\zeta^p
		 & =\beta\dot\lambda.
		\label{eq:finite_distension_flow}
	\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Because \(\tr\mbf N=0\), the first equation preserves
\(\det\bar{\mbf F}^{p}=1\), while the second evolves plastic distension.
The normalization in \eqref{eq:finite_equivalent_stress} gives
\(\dot\gamma=\dot\lambda\) during plastic loading.
Together, the two equations update the factors in
\begin{equation*}
	\mbf F
	=\left(a^e a^p\right)^{1/3}
	\bar{\mbf F}^{e}\bar{\mbf F}^{p}.
\end{equation*}

The yield condition and loading rules are
\begin{equation}
	f(\mbf M,p)=q-(\mu+\beta)p\le0,
	\qquad
	\dot\lambda\ge0,
	\qquad
	\dot\lambda f=0,
	\qquad
	\dot f=0\ \text{when}\ \dot\lambda>0.
	\label{eq:finite_loading_conditions}
\end{equation}
Substituting \eqref{eq:finite_flow_rules} and
\eqref{eq:finite_loading_conditions} into the finite-deformation plastic power
gives
\begin{align*}
	\mathcal D^p
	 & =\mbf M:
	\left[
		\dot{\bar{\mbf F}}^{p}(\bar{\mbf F}^{p})^{-1}
		\right]
	-p\frac{\dot a^p}{a^p}
	\\
	 & =q\dot\lambda-p\beta\dot\lambda
	\\
	 & =\mu p\dot\lambda
	\ge0.
\end{align*}
The dissipation proof pairs each internal rate with the stress that does work
through it.  The finite-deformation yield surface is therefore a
Drucker--Prager-type condition in \((p,q)\), with \(p\) formed from the
Kirchhoff stress and \(q\) from the Mandel-type stress that drives isochoric
plastic flow.  At small strain this surface reduces to the conventional
Cauchy-stress form.  At finite strain, a particular Drucker--Prager
implementation must express its chosen stress measure and stress update in
terms of \(\mbf M\) and \(p\) before the two formulations can be compared.

\subsection{Exact homogeneous compressive-loading example}
\label{sec:finite_example}

We check the model on a specimen that deforms uniformly, with its principal
directions fixed throughout loading. We assume no hardening and choose the
following objective isotropic elastic energy per reference volume
\begin{equation}
 \psi=G\left\|\tfrac12\log\left[(\bar{\mbf F}^e)^T
 \bar{\mbf F}^e\right]\right\|^2+\frac{K_e}{2}(\zeta^e)^2,
 \qquad \det\bar{\mbf F}^e=1.
 \label{eq:example_energy}
\end{equation}
The matrix logarithm acts on the positive-definite elastic right
Cauchy--Green tensor. This Hencky-type energy is used only on the specified
coaxial path; stability under more general loading would require a separate
analysis of the elastic energy and the coupled flow law.
The path is coaxial throughout, so let the fixed direction be
\(\mbf N=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1/2,-1/2)\), for which
\(\tr\mbf N=0\) and \(\mbf N:\mbf N=3/2\). We write the imposed path
and the integrated plastic factors as
\begin{align*}
 \mbf F(t)&=\exp\left[s(t)\mbf N-\tfrac13v(t)\mbf I\right],
 \qquad J=e^{-v}, \\
 \bar{\mbf F}^p(t)&=\exp[\gamma(t)\mbf N],
 \qquad a^p=e^{\beta\gamma}, \\
 \bar{\mbf F}^e(t)&=\exp[(s-\gamma)\mbf N],
 \qquad \zeta^e=-v-\beta\gamma.
\end{align*}
Here \(s,v,\gamma\) are dimensionless accumulated shear, compression, and
plastic shear measures, respectively, and \(\dot\gamma=\dot\lambda\) during
plastic loading. These diagonal factors commute, so the formulas are exact at
finite deformation. Differentiating the energy along the coaxial elastic
directions yields
\begin{align}
 \mbf M&=2G(s-\gamma)\mbf N,
 \qquad q=3G(s-\gamma), \label{eq:example_shear_stress}\\
 p&=K_e(v+\beta\gamma),
 \qquad \bs\tau=2G(s-\gamma)\mbf N-p\mbf I.
 \label{eq:example_pressure}
\end{align}
The formula for \(q\) assumes \(s-\gamma>0\), as verified below.
Because the elastic stretch and stress commute, the Mandel and Kirchhoff
deviatoric invariants coincide on this path. The Cauchy stress is
\(\bs\sigma=\bs\tau/J\), with Cauchy pressure \(p/J\) and
Cauchy equivalent shear stress \(q/J\).

Take \(G=10\,\mathrm{MPa}\), \(K_e=20\,\mathrm{MPa}\),
\(\mu=0.4\), and \(\beta=0.2\), so \(M=0.6\). Start stress-free with
\(\mbf F=\bar{\mbf F}^p=\mbf I\) and \(a^p=1\). We first apply elastic
hydrostatic compression to \(v_0=0.05\), \(s=0\), which gives
\(p_0=1\,\mathrm{MPa}\). At fixed \(v_0\), we then apply elastic shear to
\(s_0=Mp_0/(3G)=0.02\). The state reaches \(q_0=0.6\,\mathrm{MPa}\)
on the smooth yield surface, with \(\gamma=0\). Both preload steps are elastic
and dissipate no power.

Set \(t=0\) at the end of this preload. For \(0\le t\le1\,\mathrm{s}\),
impose \(s=s_0+\dot\varepsilon_s t\) and
\(v=v_0+\dot\varepsilon_v t\), with
\(\dot\varepsilon_s=0.1\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\) and
\(\dot\varepsilon_v=0.02\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\). Consistency and the initial
state give the exact solution
\begin{align}
 \dot\gamma&=\frac{3G\dot\varepsilon_s-MK_e\dot\varepsilon_v}
 {3G+MK_e\beta},\notag\\
 &=\frac{23}{270}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},
 \qquad \gamma(0)=0, \label{eq:example_multiplier}\\
 p(t)&=p_0+K_e(\dot\varepsilon_v+\beta\dot\gamma)t,
 \qquad q(t)=Mp(t), \label{eq:example_stress_path}\\
 \mathcal D^p(t)&=\mu p(t)\dot\gamma>0,
 \notag\\
 \int_0^t\mathcal D^p(u)\,du
 &=\mu\dot\gamma\left[p_0t+
 \tfrac12K_e(\dot\varepsilon_v+\beta\dot\gamma)t^2\right].
 \notag
\end{align}
The integral is dissipated energy per reference volume; the integration starts

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Separating the mechanisms shows where plastic work goes and how plastic shape
change is tied to volume change. Take a confined specimen that dilates as it
shears. Expansion against the confining pressure consumes part of the work
supplied by shear. With energetic hardening neglected,
\eqref{eq:small_strain_power} gives this work balance directly: the shear
power is \(q\dot\gamma\), the power needed for dilation is
\(p\dot\zeta^p\), and the remainder is dissipated. The coupled law assigns the
dilation cost through \(\beta\) and the dissipative resistance through
\(\mu\). Their sum gives the frictional yield slope \(M\). The difference
between the friction and dilation slopes therefore has a concrete physical
meaning in this specialization.

This interpretation follows the stress-dependent dissipation construction of
Collins and Houlsby (1997) \cite[Sec.~5]{collins1997}. The present kinematics
attach each rate to a finite-deformation quantity: isochoric plastic
deformation for shear and plastic distension for volume change.
Equations~\eqref{eq:finite_flow_rules} then evolve the corresponding factors
while \eqref{eq:plastic_dissipation} accounts for their power. For the
cohesionless law considered here, equal friction and dilation slopes give
\(\mu=0\): all shear power is balanced by dilation work, and the plastic
dissipation is zero. This limit concerns this particular law; an associated
model with cohesion or other dissipative resistance can dissipate positive
power.

Maximum plastic dissipation adds a stronger constitutive requirement to
nonnegative dissipation. In its classical convex form, it compares the work
of admissible stresses through a given plastic rate, with the internal state
and elastic domain held fixed. The resulting flow belongs to the normal cone
of that domain \cite{bland1957,simo1988}. The independent model has this
normality property at a fixed hardening state: its active rates combine the
outward normals of the full elastic domain, including at a corner. The
coupled model instead has a flow direction normal to the yield surface in
generalized-force space when the pressure is held fixed. In physical stress
space, a change of pressure also changes the dissipative strength \(\mu p\),
which affects the slope of the yield surface. The model therefore satisfies
nonnegative dissipation while producing non-associated flow in the physical
stresses. Classical results that rely on normality in the physical stress
require a separate assessment for this coupled law.

The work balance also explains why dilation requires coupling in this model.
Under compression, independently dissipative distension driven only by
\(-p\) compacts the material. Dilation consumes power through that same
force, so the coupled mechanism draws on the positive shear contribution.
Independent activation describes pressure-driven pore collapse or grain
crushing, where volume change has its own onset. Shared activation describes a
prescribed amount of dilation during shear. A material exhibiting both
behaviors would generally require a coupled shear-dilation mechanism together
with a separate compaction mechanism. The loading comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:loading_comparison} shows the resulting difference in stress
path and plastic volume change.

A more general distension closure would retain a physical state variable,
such as porosity, and the work done through its conjugate force. Herrmann's
\(p\)--\(\alpha\) description and Drumheller's mixture kinematics provide
physical context for such a choice \cite{herrmann1969,drumheller2000}.
The rescaling argument in Section~\ref{sec:energy_class} shows what must
change to give that variable its own stored energy. The Flory-closed example
uses conventional plastic volume and shape factors to show the forces, the
flow laws, and the evolution of the factors at finite deformation. A porosity
model would also need constitutive laws and calibration for the material.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

The finite-deformation formulation separates plastic shape change from
plastic volume change and pairs each rate with the force that does work
through it. When the mechanisms yield independently, their combined flow is
associated with the full elastic domain. When they are coupled, the flow
direction is normal to the yield surface in generalized-force space at fixed
pressure. The pressure-dependent dissipation and the dilation constraint make
the flow non-associated in physical stress space. The small-strain
construction agrees with the Collins--Houlsby model. At finite deformation, we
identify the factors that carry the plastic rates, the energy restrictions the
split requires, and the consequences of independent or shared activation. The
homogeneous example verifies the stress path, plastic determinant, volume
change, power balance, and small-strain reduction within that closure.

The demonstrated model assumes isotropy, cohesionless response, no energetic
hardening, smooth active states with \(q>0\), and monotone compression. The
exact example further assumes coaxial loading and the stated elastic energy.
Apex plastic loading, reversal, anisotropy, and independently energetic
microstructure require additional constitutive specifications.
